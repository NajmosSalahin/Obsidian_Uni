## Chapter 2: Theorems and Proofs

### Theorem 2.1

**Statement:** If the $n$-dimensional vectors $X_1, X_2,..., X_k$ form a basis of $E(k)$ ($n \geq k$), then every vector $Y$ of $E(k)$ is expressed uniquely as the linear combination $Y = a_1X_1 + a_2X_2 + ... + a_kX_k$.

PROOF:

Assume there exists another set of coefficients $b_1, b_2,... ,b_k$ such that $Y = b_1X_1 + b_2X_2 + .... + b_kX_k$.

Subtracting the two linear combinations yields:

$$(a_1 - b_1)X_1 + (a_2 - b_2)X_2 + ... + (a_k - b_k)X_k = 0$$

Since $X_1, X_2,...,X_k$ form a basis, they must be linearly independent. By Definition 1.5 of linear interdependence, the only way their linear combination can equal the zero vector is if all coefficients are zero.

Thus, we must have:

$$(a_1 - b_1)=(a_2 - b_2)= ... =(a_k - b_k)=0$$

This implies that $a_1 = b_1, a_2 = b_2,...,a_k = b_k$. Therefore, the coefficients are unique, and the linear combination $Y$ is uniquely determined once the basis vectors $X_i$ are given. $\square$

* Reverse: If one vector, $X_j$, is a linear combination of the rest, $X_j = \sum_{i \neq j} d_i X_i$, rearranging yields $X_j - \sum_{i \neq j} d_i X_i = 0$. Since the coefficient of $X_j$ is $1 \neq 0$, the set is linearly dependent (Definition 1.5).

Since $Y$ is now written entirely as a linear combination of $X_1, X_2,..., X_{k-1}$, it is linearly dependent on that reduced set. $\square$

### Theorem 2.3 (The Completing Theorem)

**Statement:** Let $E(n)$ be an $n$-dimensional Euclidean vector space and let $X_1, X_2,..., X_k$ be any $k \leq n$ nonzero linearly independent vectors of $E(n)$. Then there exist $r = n - k$ linearly independent vectors $X_{k+1}, X_{k+2},... X_n$, which together with $X_1, X_2,..., X_k$ form a basis of $E(n)$.

PROOF:

Since the $k$ vectors do not span the entire space $E(n)$, there must exist at least one nonzero vector $X_{k+1}$ in $E(n)$ that is not contained in the space generated by $X_1, X_2,...,X_k$.

Thus, the set $\{X_1, X_2,..., X_k, X_{k+1}\}$ must be linearly independent. If they were linearly dependent, there would exist coefficients $a_1, \dots, a_{k+1}$ not all zero such that:

$$a_1X_1 + a_2X_2 + ... + a_kX_k + a_{k+1}X_{k+1} = 0$$

If $a_{k+1}$ were nonzero, $X_{k+1}$ would be a linear combination of $X_1, \dots, X_k$, contradicting the choice of $X_{k+1}$. Hence, $a_{k+1}$ must be zero, which then forces $a_1 = a_2 = \dots = a_k = 0$ since $\{X_1, \dots, X_k\}$ is linearly independent.

If $k + 1 < n$, this process can be repeated $r = n - k$ times, eventually yielding a set of vectors $X_1, X_2,..., X_n$ that satisfy:

$$a_1X_1 + a_2X_2 + ... + a_n X_n = 0$$

where $a_1 = a_2 = \dots = a_n = 0$. This entire set is linearly independent and, by Definition 2.2, forms a basis of $E(n)$. $\square$

### Theorem 2.4

Statement: Let $S$ and $T$ be any two vector spaces such that $\dim(S) = s$, $\dim(T) = t$, $\dim(S \cap T) = m$, and $\dim(S \cup T) = r$. Then

$$\dim(S \cup T) = \dim(S) + \dim(T) - \dim(S \cap T)$$

PROOF:

Let $X_1, X_2, \dots, X_m$ be a basis of the intersection $S \cap T$.

Since $S \cap T$ is a subspace of both $S$ and $T$, we can define:

- A basis of $S$: $\{X_1, \dots, X_m, Y_1, \dots, Y_{s-m}\}$.
    
- A basis of $T$: $\{X_1, \dots, X_m, Z_1, \dots, Z_{t-m}\}$.
    

The union $S \cup T$ is generated by the set of all these vectors: $\{X_1, \dots, X_m, Y_1, \dots, Y_{s-m}, Z_1, \dots, Z_{t-m}\}$. To confirm this set is a basis, we show linear independence.

Consider the linear combination:

$$\sum_{i=1}^{m} a_i X_i + \sum_{j=1}^{s-m} b_j Y_j = - \sum_{k=1}^{t-m} c_k Z_k$$

The left side is a vector in $S$, and the right side is a vector in $T$. Since they are equal, they must reside in the intersection $S \cap T$. Thus, the right side must be expressible as a linear combination of the intersection basis $\{X_i\}$:

$$- \sum_{k=1}^{t-m} c_k Z_k = \sum_{i=1}^{m} d_i X_i$$

Since the combined set $\{X_1, \dots, X_m, Z_1, \dots, Z_{t-m}\}$ forms the basis of $T$ and is linearly independent, all coefficients $c_k$ and $d_i$ must be zero.

Since all $c_k = 0$, the original combination simplifies to:

$$\sum_{i=1}^{m} a_i X_i + \sum_{j=1}^{s-m} b_j Y_j = 0$$

Since $\{X_1, \dots, X_m, Y_1, \dots, Y_{s-m}\}$ forms the basis of $S$, all coefficients $a_i$ and $b_j$ must also be zero.

Since all coefficients are zero, the entire set of generators for $S \cup T$ is linearly independent. The dimension of $S \cup T$ is the total number of vectors:

$$\dim(S \cup T) = m + (s-m) + (t-m) = s + t - m$$

Substituting the dimensional notation:

$$\dim(S \cup T) = \dim(S) + \dim(T) - \dim(S \cap T)$$

$\square$

### Theorem 2.5

**Statement:** Let $X_1, X_2,...,X_r$ be any $r$ vectors in $S$. If another vector $Y$ is orthogonal to the $r$ vectors, then it is orthogonal to every vector that lies in the space generated by $X_1, X_2,..., X_r$.

PROOF:

Let $Z$ be any vector lying in the space generated by $X_1, X_2,...,X_r$. $Z$ can be written as a linear combination:

$$Z = a_1X_1 + a_2X_2 + ... + a_rX_r$$

We are given that $Y$ is orthogonal to the $r$ vectors, meaning $Y \cdot X_i = 0$ for $i=1, 2, \dots, r$.

Forming the inner product $Y \cdot Z$ yields:

$$Y \cdot Z = Y \cdot (a_1X_1 + a_2X_2 + \dots + a_rX_r)$$

Using the distributive property of the inner product (Theorem 1.2) and commutative property with scalar multiplication (Theorem 1.4):

$$Y \cdot Z = a_1(Y \cdot X_1) + a_2(Y \cdot X_2) + \dots + a_r(Y \cdot X_r)$$

Since $Y \cdot X_i = 0$ for all $i$ by assumption, it follows that $Y \cdot Z = 0$. Thus, $Y$ is orthogonal to every vector $Z$ in the space generated by $X_1, X_2,..., X_r$. $\square$

### Theorem 2.6

**Statement:** If $E(n)$ is an $n$-dimensional vector space and $E(r)$ is a subspace of dimension $r$, then $E(n)$ contains a nonzero vector $Y$ that is orthogonal to $E(r)$.

PROOF:

Let $X_1, X_2,...,X_r$ form a basis of $E(r)$. Since $r < n$, by the Completing Theorem (Theorem 2.3), $E(n)$ contains $n - r$ linearly independent vectors $X_{r+1}, X_{r+2},...,X_n$ such that $X_i$ ($i = 1, 2,...,n$) form a basis of $E(n)$.

Any vector $Y$ in $E(n)$ is a linear combination of this basis:

$$Y = a_1X_1 + \dots + a_rX_r + a_{r+1}X_{r+1} + \dots + a_nX_n$$

We seek coefficients $a_i$ such that $Y$ is orthogonal to $E(r)$, meaning $Y \cdot X_i = 0$ for $i=1, \dots, r$.

Forming the $r$ inner product equations $Y \cdot X_i = 0$ yields a system of $r$ linear equations in $n$ unknowns ($a_1, \dots, a_n$). Since $r < n$, this system must have a nonzero solution for the coefficients $a_i$ (by Theorem 3.22, related to homogeneous linear equations).

Thus, a nonzero vector $Y$ can be chosen to be orthogonal to $E(r)$. $\square$

### Theorem 2.7

**Statement:** Every vector space $E(n)$ contains exactly $n$ mutually orthogonal vectors.

PROOF:

Consider a set of $n$ mutually orthogonal, nonzero vectors $X_1, X_2,...,X_n$. We first show this set is linearly independent, proving that $E(n)$ cannot contain more than $n$ orthogonal vectors.

Consider the linear combination $a_1X_1 + a_2X_2 + ... + a_nX_n = 0$.

Forming the inner product of this sum with $X_i$ yields $a_i (X_i \cdot X_i) = 0$, because $X_i \cdot X_j = 0$ for $i \neq j$.

Since $X_i$ is nonzero, $X_i \cdot X_i = k_i \neq 0$. Therefore, we must have $a_i = 0$ for all $i$, meaning the set $\{X_i\}$ is linearly independent.

Now, assume $E(n)$ contains at most $m < n$ orthogonal vectors $X_1, X_2,...,X_m$. These $m$ linearly independent vectors form a basis of the $m$-dimensional subspace $E(m)$. By Theorem 2.6, there must exist a vector $X^*$ in $E(n)$ that is orthogonal to $E(m)$, and thus orthogonal to $X_1, X_2,...,X_m$. This contradicts the assumption that $m$ was the maximum number of orthogonal vectors.

Continuing this process, we conclude that $E(n)$ contains exactly $n$ orthogonal vectors. $\square$

### Theorem 2.8

**Statement:** Let $S$ be a vector space and $T$ any subspace of $S$. Then the direct sum $T \oplus T^{\perp} = S$ where $T^{\perp}$ is the orthogonal complement of $T \subset S$.

PROOF:

Let $S$ be an $n$-dimensional vector space. By Theorem 2.7, $S$ contains $n$ orthogonal basis vectors $E_1, E_2,..., E_n$. We choose the basis such that the first $r = \dim(T)$ vectors, $E_1, E_2,...,E_r$, form the basis of $T$.

Let $Y$ be any vector in $T^{\perp}$. Since $Y \in S$, $Y = y_1E_1 + y_2E_2 + ... + y_nE_n$.

Since $Y \in T^{\perp}$, $Y$ must be perpendicular to every vector in $T$, specifically the basis vectors $E_1, E_2,..., E_r$ (Definition 2.7).

Forming the inner products $Y \cdot E_i$ for $i=1, \dots, r$ (and assuming the $E_i$ are orthonormal, $E_i \cdot E_i = 1$) yields $y_1 = y_2 = \dots = y_r = 0$.

Therefore, $Y$ is given by $Y = (0, 0,..., 0, y_{r+1}, y_{r+2},...,y_n)$, meaning $T^{\perp}$ is spanned by $E_{r+1}, \dots, E_n$. Thus $\dim(T^{\perp}) = n - r$.

Since every vector in $T$ is orthogonal to every vector in $T^{\perp}$ (Definition 2.6, Definition 2.7), their intersection is only the zero vector: $T \cap T^{\perp} = 0$.

By the definition of the direct sum (Section 2.4, related to Theorem 2.4), $T \oplus T^{\perp}$ has dimension $\dim(T) + \dim(T^{\perp}) = r + (n-r) = n$. Since $S$ has dimension $n$, $T \oplus T^{\perp} = S$. $\square$

### Example 2.1 (Examples of Vector Spaces)

**Question/Context:** Provide examples of sets of vectors that qualify as a vector space (Definition 2.1).

Answer (Solution):

i. The (infinite) set of all $n$-component vectors $(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n)$ forms a vector space, because vector sums and scalar products defined for $n$-component vectors result again in an $n$-component vector.

ii. The zero vector $\mathbf{0} = (0, 0, \dots, 0)$ forms a vector space by itself, since the sum of two zero vectors ($0+0 = 0$) and the scalar product of a zero vector ($k0 = 0$) again yield the zero vector for any scalar $k$.

### Example 2.3 (Orthogonal Projection onto a Vector)

**Question:** Find the orthogonal projection ($\hat{Y}$) of the vector $Y = (2,7,1)$ onto the vector $X = (5,6,4)$.

Answer (Solution):

The projection vector $\hat{Y}$ is proportional to $X$, so $\hat{Y} = \beta X$. The coefficient of projection $\beta$ is determined by $\beta = (X \cdot Y) / (X \cdot X)$.

- Calculate the inner product $X \cdot Y$: $X \cdot Y = 2(5) + 7(6) + 1(4) = 10 + 42 + 4 = 56$.
    
- Calculate the squared length $X \cdot X = ||X||^2$: $X \cdot X = 5^2 + 6^2 + 4^2 = 25 + 36 + 16 = 77$.
    
- Calculate the coefficient $\beta$: $\beta = 56/77 \approx 0.727$.
    
- The projection vector is $\hat{Y} = 0.73X$ (using the rounded value provided by the source).

### Example 2.4 (Orthogonal Projection onto a Plane)

**Question:** Find the orthogonal projection ($\hat{Y}$) of $Y = (7,7,8)$ onto the plane spanned by $X_1 = (5,12,1)$ and $X_2 = (9,5,1)$.

Answer (Solution):

The orthogonal projection $\hat{Y}$ must lie in the plane, so $\hat{Y} = \beta_1 X_1 + \beta_2 X_2$. The error vector $e = Y - \hat{Y}$ must be orthogonal to both $X_1$ and $X_2$ ($X_1 \cdot e = 0$ and $X_2 \cdot e = 0$).

We solve the system of equations derived from $X_i \cdot Y = X_i \cdot \hat{Y}$:

$$\begin{aligned} X_1 \cdot Y &= \beta_1 (X_1 \cdot X_1) + \beta_2 (X_1 \cdot X_2) \\ X_2 \cdot Y &= \beta_1 (X_2 \cdot X_1) + \beta_2 (X_2 \cdot X_2) \end{aligned}$$

The calculated inner products are $Y \cdot X_1 = 127$, $Y \cdot X_2 = 106$, $X_1 \cdot X_1 = 170$, $X_1 \cdot X_2 = 106$, and $X_2 \cdot X_2 = 107$.

The system is:

$$\begin{aligned} 127 &= 170\beta_1 + 106\beta_2 \\ 106 &= 106\beta_1 + 107\beta_2 \end{aligned}$$

Solving this system yields the coefficients: $\beta_1 = 0.339$ and $\beta_2 = 0.655$.

The resulting projection vector is:

$$\hat{Y} = 0.339(5,12,1) + 0.655(9,5,1) = (7.16, 7.42, 0.40)$$

## Gram-Schmidt Orthogonalization

The **Gram-Schmidt Orthogonalization** method constructs an orthogonal basis $Z_1, Z_2,..., Z_r$ for a vector space $E(r)$, given an initial basis of $r$ linearly independent vectors $X_1, X_2,..., X_r$.

The procedure combines two key results:

1. An $r$-dimensional space $E(r)$ must contain exactly $r$ orthogonal vectors.
    
2. The vector resulting from subtracting a vector $Y$ from its orthogonal projection $\hat{Y}$ (i.e., $Y - \hat{Y}$) is always orthogonal to the subspace onto which the projection occurred.
    

The method is sequential, relying on orthogonal projection:

- **Select the first vector:** The first orthogonal vector is set equal to the first given vector: $Z_1 = X_1$.
    
- Determine subsequent vectors: Each subsequent orthogonal vector $Z_i$ is constructed by taking $X_i$ and
    
    subtracting its orthogonal projections onto all previously constructed orthogonal vectors $Z_j$ ($j < i$).
    
- **Second vector example:** The second orthogonal vector is $Z_2 = X_2 - \hat{Y}$, where $\hat{Y}$ is the projection of $X_2$ onto $X_1$ (which is $Z_1$). The coefficient of projection is calculated as $\beta_1 = (X_2 \cdot X_1)/(X_1 \cdot X_1)$.
    
- **Result:** The resulting set of vectors $\{Z_1, Z_2, \dots, Z_r\}$ is mutually orthogonal. This ensures that the vectors form an orthogonal basis.
    
- **Normalization:** The vectors are typically normalized to unit length afterward to form an orthonormal basis.
    

The orthogonality of this process can be demonstrated using orthogonal projection matrices $Q_i = (I - P_i)$, which also prove useful when solving systems of simultaneous linear equations.

## Chapter 2: Definitions

### Definition 2.1 (Vector Space)

Any set of vectors that is closed under vector addition and scalar multiplication is known as a **vector space**. Since for any vector $X$, we have $-X = (-1)X$, it follows that a vector space is also closed with respect to subtraction.

---

### Definition 2.2 (Basis)

Let $X_1, X_2, ..., X_k$ represent a generating system of a Euclidean vector space. If a subset of $1 \leq r \leq k$ vectors in the generating system is linearly independent, then it is known as a **basis** of $E(r)$.

---

### Definition 2.3 (Dimension)

The **dimension** of a vector space $E(k)$ is equal to the maximum number of linearly independent vectors in $E(k)$. For a vector space of dimension $k$, every generating system of $E(k)$ can contain at most $k$ linearly independent vectors, and every basis of $E(k)$ will contain exactly $k$ vectors.

---

### Definition 2.4 (Subspace)

Consider two vector spaces $S$ and $T$ such that all vectors contained in $S$ are also contained in $T$, but not all vectors of $T$ are necessarily contained in $S$. Then $S$ is said to be a **proper subspace** of $T$, written $S \subset T$. More generally, $S$ is a **subspace** of $T$ when $S \subseteq T$; that is, both $S$ and $T$ may contain identical vectors.

---

### Definition 2.5 (Intersection and Sum/Union)

Let $S$ and $T$ denote any two vector spaces. Then the following applies:

1. The vector space consisting of all vectors common to both $S$ and $T$ is called the **intersection** of $S$ and $T$, written $S \cap T$.
    
2. The vector space consisting of all vectors $X_1 + X_2$, where $X_1$ lies in $S$ ($X_1 \in S$) and $X_2$ lies in $T$ ($X_2 \in T$), is called the **sum** or the **union** of $S$ and $T$, written $S \cup T$.
    

---

### Definition 2.6 (Mutually Orthogonal Vector Spaces)

The vector spaces $S$ and $T$ are said to be **mutually orthogonal** if every vector of $S$ is orthogonal to every vector in $T$.

---

### Definition 2.7 (Orthogonal Complement)

Let $S$ be a subspace of $E(n)$. Then the set of all vectors in $E(n)$ that are perpendicular to $S$ is known as the **orthogonal complement** of $S$, denoted as $S^{\perp}$.

---

### Definition 2.8 (Minimizing Vector)

Let $S$ be a Euclidean vector space, $T$ a subspace of $S$, and $Y$ any vector in $S$. Then if there exists a vector $\hat{Y} \in T$ such that $||Y - \hat{Y}|| \leq ||Y - Z||$ for any other vector $Z \in T$, then vector $\hat{Y}$ is said to be the **minimizing vector** (of the distance) between $Y$ and $T$.
