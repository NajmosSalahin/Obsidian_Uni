### **Part B: MACROECONOMICS (Conceptual Questions)**

1. What is national income?

National Income is the total money value of all final goods and services produced by a country's normal residents during a given period, usually one year. From an income perspective, it represents the sum of all factor incomes (wages, rent, interest, and profit) earned by the factors of production owned by the country's citizens.

**2. Define — GDP, GNP, NNP, NI, PI, DI**

- **GDP (Gross Domestic Product):** The total market value of all final goods and services produced within the _geographic borders_ of a country during a specific period, regardless of who owns the production assets.

- **GNP (Gross National Product):** The total market value of all final goods and services produced by the _nationals (citizens)_ of a country, regardless of where they are located. Formula: $GNP = GDP + \text{Net Factor Income from Abroad (NFIA)}$.

- **NNP (Net National Product):** GNP adjusted for the depreciation of capital stock (wear and tear). Formula: $NNP = GNP - \text{Depreciation}$.
  
- **NI (National Income):** Technically NNP at factor cost. It is the actual income earned by factors of production. Formula: $NI = NNP_{\text{market price}} - \text{Indirect Taxes} + \text{Subsidies}$.
  
- **PI (Personal Income):** The income actually received by households before paying personal income taxes. It includes transfer payments but excludes retained corporate earnings.
 
- **DI (Disposable Income):** The income households have available for spending or saving after income taxes are paid. Formula: $DI = PI - \text{Personal Taxes}$.
  

3. Measurement of national income.

Economists measure National Income using three approaches, which theoretically yield the same result:

1. **Product (Output) Method:** Summing the market value of all final goods and services produced (or summing the "value added" at each stage of production to avoid double counting).
    
2. **Income Method:** Summing all incomes earned by factors of production (Wages + Rent + Interest + Profit).
    
3. **Expenditure Method:** Summing the total spending on final goods ($C + I + G + NX$).
    

4. What is the consumption function?

The consumption function (or Propensity to Consume) describes the functional relationship between total consumption ($C$) and gross national income ($Y$). It is often expressed linearly as:

$$C = a + bY$$

Where $a$ is autonomous consumption (survival spending when income is zero) and $b$ is the Marginal Propensity to Consume.

**5. Define APC and MPC?**

- APC (Average Propensity to Consume): The ratio of total consumption expenditure to total income. It represents the percentage of income spent.
    
    $$APC = \frac{C}{Y}$$
    
- MPC (Marginal Propensity to Consume): The ratio of the change in consumption to the change in income. It measures how much of an extra dollar earned is spent.
    
    $$MPC = \frac{\Delta C}{\Delta Y}$$
    

**6. What are the differences between APC and MPC?**

- **Scope:** APC measures the _average_ spending behavior at a specific income level, while MPC measures the _incremental_ spending behavior resulting from income growth.
    
- **Behavior:** In the short run, as income rises, APC tends to fall (people save a larger portion of total income), whereas MPC is often assumed to be constant in simple linear models (Keynesian psychological law).
    
- **Value:** APC can be greater than 1 (if spending exceeds income via borrowing), but MPC is always between 0 and 1.
    

**7. What are the difficulties of measuring national income in Bangladesh or developing countries?**

- **Non-Monetized Sector:** A large portion of transactions in rural areas (e.g., barter systems, subsistence farming) does not involve money and is omitted from GDP.
    
- **Lack of Reliable Data:** Small producers and the informal sector often do not maintain proper accounts, leading to estimation errors.
    
- **Underground Economy:** Illegal activities or unreported legal activities (to avoid taxes) are not captured.
    
- **Illiteracy:** Makes gathering accurate survey data difficult.
    
- **Double Counting:** Difficulty in distinguishing between intermediate and final goods in complex supply chains without proper records.
    

8. How can we measure the national income by expenditure method?

We sum the four main components of spending:

$$Y = C + I + G + (X - M)$$

- $C$: Personal Consumption Expenditure.
    
- $I$: Gross Private Domestic Investment.
    
- $G$: Government Consumption and Gross Investment.
    
- $(X - M)$: Net Exports (Exports minus Imports).
    

9. What is investment?

In macroeconomics, investment refers to the purchase of new capital goods (machinery, buildings, equipment) and changes in inventories that are used to produce other goods and services in the future. It is not purchasing stocks or bonds (which is financial investment), but the creation of real productive assets.

**10. Determinants of investment.**

- **Interest Rates:** The cost of borrowing. There is an inverse relationship; higher rates discourage investment.
    
- **Marginal Efficiency of Capital (MEC):** The expected rate of return on a new investment.
    
- **Business Expectations:** "Animal spirits" or confidence regarding future demand and economic climate.
    
- **Technological Advancement:** New tech necessitates new capital equipment.
    
- **Government Policy:** Tax incentives or subsidies for capital spending.
    

11. What do you mean by GDP Deflator?

The GDP Deflator is a price index that measures the average level of prices of all new, domestically produced, final goods and services in an economy. It indicates how much of the increase in GDP is due to higher prices (inflation) rather than higher output.

$$GDP \text{ Deflator} = \frac{\text{Nominal GDP}}{\text{Real GDP}} \times 100$$

12. What is the investment multiplier?

The investment multiplier represents the factor by which National Income changes in response to a change in investment. Because one person's spending is another's income, an initial injection of investment leads to a larger final increase in total income.

$$K = \frac{1}{1 - MPC} \quad \text{or} \quad K = \frac{1}{MPS}$$

**14. The Land of Milk and Honey**

- **a. Compute Nominal GDP, Real GDP, and GDP Deflator (Base: 2010)**
    
    - **2010:**
        
        - Nominal: $(1 \times 100) + (2 \times 50) = \$200$
            
        - Real (Base 2010): Same as Nominal = **$200**
            
        - Deflator: $(200/200) \times 100 = \mathbf{100}$
            
    - **2011:**
        
        - Nominal: $(1 \times 200) + (2 \times 100) = \$400$
            
        - Real (use 2010 prices): $(1 \times 200) + (2 \times 100) = \mathbf{\$400}$
            
        - Deflator: $(400/400) \times 100 = \mathbf{100}$
            
    - **2012:**
        
        - Nominal: $(2 \times 200) + (4 \times 100) = \$800$
            
        - Real (use 2010 prices): $(1 \times 200) + (2 \times 100) = \mathbf{\$400}$
            
        - Deflator: $(800/400) \times 100 = \mathbf{200}$
            
- **b. Percentage change in Nominal GDP and GDP Deflator**
    
    - **2011:** Nominal GDP rose by 100% ($200 \to 400$). Deflator rose by 0% (constant prices).
        
    - **2012:** Nominal GDP rose by 100% ($400 \to 800$). Deflator rose by 100% ($100 \to 200$).
        
- **c. Identify the variable that does not change.**
    
    - In 2011, **prices** did not change (Deflator remained 100).
        
    - In 2012, **output (quantity)** did not change (Real GDP remained $400).

**16. US GDP Growth (1999-2009)**

- **a. Nominal GDP Growth:** $\frac{14,256 - 9,353}{9,353} \times 100 = \mathbf{52.4\%}$
    
- **b. Deflator Growth (Inflation):** $\frac{109.8 - 86.8}{86.8} \times 100 = \mathbf{26.5\%}$
    
- **c. Real GDP Growth:**
    
    - Real GDP 1999 = $9,353 / 0.868 = 10,775$
        
    - Real GDP 2009 = $14,256 / 1.098 = 12,983$
        
    - Growth = $\frac{12,983 - 10,775}{10,775} \times 100 = \mathbf{20.5\%}$
        
- **d. Explanation:** Nominal GDP growth was much higher than Real GDP growth because a significant portion of the increase was simply due to rising prices (inflation), not just increased production.
    

**17. Women in the Labor Force**

- **a. Effect on GDP:** The rise in women's labor-force participation **increased GDP**. Activities that were previously non-market (home cooking, cleaning, child-rearing) shifted to market services (restaurants, cleaning services, daycares), which are tracked in GDP.
    
- **b. Well-being vs. GDP:** A measure including home production would show a **smaller increase** than standard GDP. While market output rose, home production fell. The standard GDP calculation "double counts" the gain by ignoring the loss of home production.
    
- **c. Other aspects:** Aspects include increased stress, less leisure time, or changes in child development environments. It is theoretically possible but practically very difficult to construct a single measure including these due to the subjectivity of valuing "leisure" or "stress" in monetary terms.

**19. Value Added Problem**

- a. What is GDP?
    
    GDP is $180. This is the value of the final good (bread) sold to the consumer. The wheat and flour are intermediate goods and are not counted separately to avoid double counting.
    
- **b. Value Added of each producer:**
    
    - **Farmer:** Sold $100 - Bought $0 = **$100**
        
    - **Miller:** Sold $150 - Bought $100 (Wheat) = **$50**
        
    - **Baker:** Sold $180 - Bought $150 (Flour) = **$30**
        
- **c. Total Value Added vs. GDP:**
    
    - Total Value Added = $100 + 50 + 30 = \mathbf{\$180}$.
        
    - This is **equal** to the GDP calculated in part (a).
        
    - **Conclusion:** This example illustrates the **Value Added (or Production) Method** of calculating GDP. It proves that summing the value added at each stage of production yields the same result as summing the expenditure on final goods.
