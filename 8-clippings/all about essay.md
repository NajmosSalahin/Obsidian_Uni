## 1. Why write essays?

"Essay" comes from the French _essayer_ — to try. That's the actual point of the form: an essay isn't a monument to a conclusion you already reached, it's the record of you testing whether an idea holds up.

That matters because thinking silently in your head is unreliable. You can feel certain about something and still be wrong, because a vague impression never has to survive contact with a full sentence. The moment you try to write it down in a way a stranger could follow, the gaps show up — a claim with no evidence, a step in the logic that doesn't connect, a conclusion that doesn't actually follow from what came before. Writing is one of the few activities that forces this kind of scrutiny on your own thinking, in real time, before anyone else even sees it.

It's also why essays outlast the moment they're written in. A conversation evaporates. An essay is a fixed, inspectable object — you can reread it in five years and argue with your past self, which is a strange and genuinely useful thing to be able to do. That's basically why the form has stuck around for centuries, from Montaigne trying to figure out how to live well, to a blog post trying to figure out why a model keeps overfitting.

## 2. How essays help in everyday life

Outside school or blogging, this shows up more often than people expect:

- **Decisions.** Writing a short "should I do X" essay to yourself — the case for, the case against, what you actually believe once it's laid out — is structured deliberation. It's more reliable than mulling something over in your head, where it's easy to quietly dodge the strongest counterargument.
- **Work communication.** A persuasive email, a project proposal, a performance review — these are all small essays with a claim, evidence, and a structure that either holds together or doesn't. People who practice essay writing tend to write clearer emails, because they've practiced building an argument instead of just dumping information.
- **Hard conversations.** Essay writing trains you to represent a position fairly before you respond to it — a one-sided essay falls apart under its own weight. That habit carries over: understanding the other side before answering makes you calmer and more convincing in real disagreements, instead of just reactive.
- **Processing hard experiences.** Reflective writing has measurable psychological benefits — this is the basis of expressive-writing research going back to psychologist James Pennebaker, which found that writing about a difficult experience, even for a short stretch of days, tends to improve mood and stress levels. Giving an experience some narrative shape takes away some of its power to just churn in the background.

## 3. How essays help you build a second brain

Quick note before this one and the next two: "second brain," PKM, and Zettelkasten get used almost interchangeably, but they're not the same thing — PKM is the general practice, "second brain" is Tiago Forte's popular branding of one approach to it, and Zettelkasten is a specific, older method within it. I'll try to say something distinct in each rather than repeat the same point three times.

The second-brain idea is usually described with the acronym **CODE**: Capture, Organize, Distill, Express. Most people who try to build one get very good at the first step — clipping articles, highlighting books, saving tweets — and never reach the last one. The result is a large, tidy archive of stuff you'll basically never look at again, because nothing forces you to.

Essays are what force "Express." You can't write even a short essay from your notes without pulling specific ones out, checking whether they actually say what you remember, and stitching them into an argument. That's the moment a second brain stops being a filing cabinet and starts being useful. And if you sit down to write and discover your notes don't have what you need, that's valuable information — it tells you exactly what you're capturing wrong, which improves the next round. Essays are the feedback loop that keeps the whole system honest.

## 4. How essays help in PKM (personal knowledge management)

Where the second-brain answer was about the capture-to-express pipeline, PKM as a general practice is more about what happens to information after it's captured — and the key idea is retrieval.

Memory research is fairly consistent here: recognizing information again barely helps you learn it, while retrieving it — pulling it out of memory and using it, unprompted — is one of the most effective ways to actually cement it. Writing an essay from your notes is about as hard a retrieval exercise as exists: you're not just recalling a fact, you're recalling it, checking whether it's still true, and figuring out how it connects to two or three other things you also had to recall.

Essays also fix PKM's most common failure mode, which is that most systems are built to store information but not to reveal its structure. Two notes can sit in different folders for years without you noticing they're about the same underlying idea — until you try to write something that needs both, and the connection becomes obvious. Once written, that essay becomes a new, high-quality node in your system: denser and easier to find again than the raw notes it came from. In a real sense, essays are the reason to bother with a PKM system at all.

## 5. How essays help in Zettelkasten

Of the three, this is the most direct relationship, because the Zettelkasten method was essentially built around writing.

The system — a "slip-box" of small, atomic notes, one idea each, densely linked to related notes — comes from the sociologist Niklas Luhmann, who used a personal archive of around 90,000 individual notes to produce more than 70 books and roughly 600 publications total over a 40-year career. Sönke Ahrens' book on the method makes an argument worth sitting with: writing isn't something that happens after you've finished understanding a topic — it's the process through which real understanding happens in the first place. If that's true, a note-taking system's whole job is to make writing as frictionless as possible.

That's what the linking does. In a mature Zettelkasten, you don't sit down to write with a blank page and a topic — you follow an existing thread of linked notes, and that thread is already close to an outline. The hard part of essay writing — figuring out what goes where — is partly solved in advance, because the order already exists as a path through the network.

The reverse is just as useful: trying to write an essay is the best way to find out whether your Zettelkasten actually has real connections in it, or whether it's a pile of isolated quotes that happen to live in the same app. If you can't find a path through your notes, that's the system telling you where the real thinking still needs to happen.

## 6. How essays help a data science student

Data science has a well-known failure mode: technically strong work that never gets used, because it never gets understood by the people who'd need to act on it. Essay writing is direct training against that:

- **The structure is the same.** A good analysis and a good essay follow the same shape — a clear question, evidence, an honest treatment of what could be wrong with the conclusion, and a specific claim at the end. Practice one and the other gets easier.
- **It catches fuzzy understanding early.** Writing one clear paragraph on why you used a log transform, or what a p-value does and doesn't tell you, exposes immediately whether you understand the concept or you're pattern-matching code from a previous project — the same mechanism as the Feynman technique.
- **It builds a portfolio that gets read.** Hiring managers rarely review someone's code line by line, but a well-written post walking through a project — the problem, the approach, what didn't work, what the result meant — is exactly the artifact that gets a callback.
- **It's most of what "communicating results to stakeholders" means.** Model cards, READMEs, technical reports, and the "walk me through this project" interview answer are all essay-shaped: they need a thesis, not just a wall of numbers.

For a data science student, essay writing isn't a soft-skill side project — it's the skill that decides whether the modeling work you're good at actually reaches anyone.

## 7. How to write an essay

There's no single correct process, but most reliable approaches follow roughly the same shape: The order matters more than any individual step. Most weak essays come from jumping straight from a rough draft to proofreading, skipping the part where you actually revise the argument itself.

1. **Understand the real question:** Restate the prompt or your own guiding question in your own words before doing anything else. Most weak essays answer a slightly different question than the one that was actually asked.
2. **Gather more material than you need:** Collect notes, examples, and evidence broadly first. It's much easier to cut good material later than to write with too little to work with. 
3. **Free write to find your thesis:** Write fast and messily about the topic without editing. Your real thesis usually shows up after you've written your way toward it, not before you start.
4. **Build a loose outline:** Order your points by logic rather than the order you thought of them. Decide which piece of evidence is strongest and which counterargument you actually need to address.
5. **Write a fast, rough first draft:** Get the whole thing down without stopping to polish sentences. Momentum matters more than quality here, and a complete bad draft beats one perfect paragraph.
6. **Revise the argument first:** Re-read for logic gaps, missing evidence, and a wandering thesis before you touch word choice. Structural problems make it a waste of effort to polish around them.
7. **Edit for clarity and voice:** Now tighten sentences, cut filler words, and read for rhythm. This is where the writing actually starts to sound like you.
8. **Proofread last:** Check grammar, spelling, and citations only once the structure is locked, so you're not polishing sentences you might still delete.
## 8. How to improve your essay writing

Improvement here comes far more from doing than from studying advice about doing — but a few habits reliably speed it up:

- **Write a lot, badly, on purpose at first.** Volume beats polish early on; most of the actual improvement comes from reps, not from reading more craft advice (this one included).
- **Read like a writer, not just a reader.** When a sentence or paragraph works, stop and figure out why — how it opened, where the turn happened, how it landed — instead of just moving on.
- **Get a real reader, not just a grade.** "I got confused here" or "I stopped believing you in paragraph two" is worth more than "good job." Someone who tells you exactly where they lost the thread is more useful than ten people who say it was fine.
- **Let a draft sit before revising it.** A day or two of distance is often enough to read your own writing the way a stranger would, which is the only way to actually catch its problems.
- **Read it out loud.** Awkward rhythm and unclear sentences are almost always obvious the moment you hear them, even when they look fine on the page.
- **Track your own recurring notes.** If a teacher or editor keeps flagging the same issue — a buried thesis, a weak ending, too many qualifiers — write that pattern down and check for it specifically on your next draft, instead of hoping general practice fixes it.
- **Set constraints on purpose.** A hard word limit or a "one thesis only" rule forces decisions that open-ended writing lets you avoid.

## 9. How to start writing essays when you've never written one

This is different from the next question — this one is about building the habit and identity of "someone who writes," before worrying about any single essay being good.

- **Lower the stakes all the way.** Write somewhere no one will read it first — a private note, a journal — so you're not performing for an audience before you've even figured out what you think.
- **Start absurdly small.** A 150-word essay on something concrete ("why this coffee shop is better than that one," "what actually annoyed me today and why") is a completely different, more doable task than "write an essay about creativity."
- **Imitate before you invent.** Pick a short essay or post you genuinely enjoyed and consciously copy its shape — how long the intro is, where the main claim lands, how many examples it uses — with your own content. This is a normal way to learn a form, not cheating.
- **Write from a question, not a "topic."** Topics are vague and intimidating; a specific question ("why do I keep putting this off?") gives you something to actually chase down the page.
- **Use talking as a bridge.** If writing feels foreign, explain your idea out loud for two minutes like you're talking to a friend, then write down roughly what you said. Most people can already talk in coherent arguments — that skill transfers.
- **Expect the first 20 or so to be bad.** They will be, for everyone. That's not a sign you're "not a writer," it's just what the start of any skill looks like.

## 10. How to start an essay from a blank page

This assumes you're past the identity question above and just staring at an actual blank page for one specific essay. A few things reliably break the freeze:

- **Don't start with the introduction.** Write the section you understand best first, usually somewhere in the middle, and write the intro last, once you actually know what you're introducing.
- **Freewrite for ten minutes with no editing.** Write everything you know or think about the topic without stopping to fix anything, even if you repeat yourself. Treat it as raw material, not a draft.
- **Give yourself explicit permission to write badly.** A genuinely bad, rough version gets words on the page, and editing an existing bad paragraph is much easier than inventing a good one from nothing.
- **Explain it to a specific person.** Write the way you'd actually explain the idea out loud to a smart friend who doesn't know the topic yet. You can make it more formal later.
- **Argue with a position you disagree with.** Sometimes it's easier to start by attacking a claim you don't believe than to defend the "real" one you do — it gets words moving, and you can pivot once you have momentum.
- **Set an absurdly low bar for the first ten minutes.** "Just write five bad sentences" is a target you can actually hit, and hitting it is usually enough to break the freeze.
- **Once you have anything, do a reverse outline.** Look at what you've written and jot down, in one line each, what each paragraph is actually doing. This usually reveals the real structure hiding in your rough draft, which you can then reorder.
